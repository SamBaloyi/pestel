Unit 5 — Cost Determination
Chapters on manufacturing cost flow · Learning outcomes: prepare manufacturing cost statement and income statement

The Big Picture — Why Does This Exist?

A manufacturing company doesn't just buy and sell — it transforms raw materials into finished goods. We need a way to track exactly how much it costs to produce those goods, because that cost becomes the "cost of sales" on the income statement. The manufacturing cost statement gives us that number.

Think of it as a funnel: raw materials go in, get worked on (adding labour and overhead costs), and finished goods come out. At each stage there may be partially-finished units (WIP) that we need to account for.

5.1Prime Costs — The Direct Core of Manufacturing▼

Prime costs are the costs you can directly trace to the product — materials used in making it, and labour that worked directly on it. Everything else is overhead.

Definition
Prime Costs = Direct Materials Used + Direct Labour

Part 1: Direct Materials Used

You need to distinguish between materials purchased and materials used. If you bought R920 000 of materials but have R165 000 left at year-end, you only used R755 000... wait, also account for what you had at the start.

Direct Materials Used
Opening RM + Purchases − Closing RM = Materials Used

Worked example (Tia Ltd):

Opening raw materials (1 Jan)120 000
Add: Purchases during year890 000
Less: Closing raw materials (30 Jun)(165 000)
Direct Materials Used845 000

Part 2: Direct Labour

This is the wages of workers who physically make the product. When you're given a production rate, you calculate hours first.

When production rate given
Hours = Units produced ÷ Units per hour
DL Cost = Hours × Rate per worker (calculate separately for each worker)

Worked example (Sweet Sweets Ltd — 10 000 bars, 2 000/hr, mixer R100/hr, QC R140/hr):

Hours = 10 000 ÷ 2 0005 hours
Mixer: 5 hrs × R100500
Quality controller: 5 hrs × R140700
Total Direct Labour1 200
Exam technique: Always show the hours calculation as a separate step. Examiners want to see the working, not just the final number.
5.2Manufacturing Overheads — What's In, What's Out▼

Manufacturing overheads are indirect factory costs — they support production but can't be traced to a specific unit. The key word is factory — only factory-related costs qualify.

✅ Manufacturing Overheads (include)
Factory rent
Factory electricity & water
Indirect materials (screws, glue, nuts)
Indirect factory wages (supervisors, cleaners)
Factory equipment depreciation
Factory insurance
❌ NOT Manufacturing Overheads
Admin & secretarial wages
CEO/management salaries
Office rent
Selling & distribution costs
Marketing expenses
Interest on loans
Classic exam trap: Sun & Moon Ltd example includes "admin & secretarial wages R33 680" in the list. Many students include it as an overhead. It's NOT — it's a non-manufacturing cost excluded from the cost statement entirely.
5.3Work-in-Progress — Why It Matters▼

At any point in time, some units are partially complete — they've been started but not finished. We call these Work-in-Progress (WIP). The WIP adjustment ensures we only count the cost of units that were actually completed in the period.

Manufacturing Costs Incurred

All costs actually incurred this period: prime costs + overheads

+ Opening WIP (costs carried forward)

These are partly-done units from last period — their costs are already spent, now we need to finish them

− Closing WIP (costs deferred to next period)

Units started but not finished — their costs can't count as "manufactured" yet

= Cost of Goods Manufactured

The total cost of units actually completed this period

Think of it like this: you bake 100 cakes but 10 are still in the oven at month-end. Your "manufactured" figure is for the 90 completed ones. The 10 in-progress carry their costs into next month.

Unit 6 — CVP Analysis
Cost-Volume-Profit Analysis · Break-even · Contribution · Margin of Safety · Sensitivity

What CVP Analysis Is Asking

CVP analysis answers one fundamental question: how does changing volume, price, or cost affect profit? Management uses it to plan: how many units must we sell to break even? To make a target profit? How much risk buffer do we have?

The whole framework rests on one concept: contribution.

6.1Contribution — The Engine of CVP▼

Contribution is what's left from the selling price after paying variable costs. It contributes first to covering fixed costs — once those are covered, every rand of contribution becomes profit.

Contribution
Contribution per unit = Selling Price − Variable Cost per unit
Total Contribution = Contribution per unit × Units sold
Profit = Total Contribution − Fixed Costs
Common mistake: Fixed costs are NEVER subtracted when calculating contribution per unit. Fixed costs only come in when calculating total profit or break-even.

What counts as a variable cost?

  • ✓Purchase/supplier price per unit
  • ✓Packaging per unit
  • ✓Direct materials per unit
  • ✓Direct labour per unit
  • ✓Import duty per unit (if % of purchase price)
  • ✗Rent, salaries, insurance, depreciation — these are FIXED, not variable

MacBurgers example from slides

Selling price120
Variable costs: ingredients R65 + labour R35(100)
Contribution per unit20
6.2Break-Even Point — Units and Rands▼

At break-even, profit = zero. Total contribution exactly equals fixed costs. Every unit beyond break-even generates pure profit (at the contribution rate).

Break-Even
BEP (units) = Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution per unit
BEP (rands) = Fixed Costs ÷ CM Ratio
CM Ratio = Contribution ÷ Selling Price × 100

MacBurgers full example

1
BEP (units) = R550 000 ÷ R20 = 27 500 units
2
CM Ratio = R20 ÷ R120 × 100 = 16.67%
3
BEP (rands) = R550 000 ÷ 16.67% = R3 299 940 (check: 27 500 × R120 = R3 300 000 ✓)
Always round UP at break-even. If BEP = 74.19 units, the answer is 75. At 74 units, your contribution is R45 880 — R120 short of covering R46 000 fixed costs. You only break even at 75.
6.3Target Sales Volume — Hitting a Profit Goal▼

Management often wants to know: how many units must we sell to earn a specific profit? The formula is identical to BEP but treats target profit exactly like a fixed cost — it needs to be "covered" just like rent does.

Target Sales Units
(Fixed Costs + Target Profit) ÷ Contribution per unit

MacBurgers: target profit R180 000

1
(R550 000 + R180 000) ÷ R20 = R730 000 ÷ R20 = 36 500 units

ShoeShine: target profit R80 000

1
(R46 000 + R80 000) ÷ R620 = R126 000 ÷ R620 = 203.2 → 204 units (round up)
Sanity check: 204 units × R620 contribution = R126 480. Less fixed costs R46 000 = R80 480. Close to R80 000 ✓ (the extra comes from rounding up).
6.4Margin of Safety — Your Risk Buffer▼

The margin of safety answers: how far can sales drop before we start losing money? It's the gap between actual sales and the break-even point. A high MoS% means you're safely above break-even.

Margin of Safety
MoS (units) = Actual units − BEP units
MoS (rands) = Actual Revenue − BEP Revenue
MoS % = MoS (rands) ÷ Actual Revenue × 100

MacBurgers: 32 000 units actually sold, BEP 27 500

Actual revenue (32 000 × R120)3 840 000
BEP revenue (27 500 × R120)(3 300 000)
Margin of Safety (rands)540 000
MoS % (R540k ÷ R3 840k × 100)14.06%

ShoeShine: 300 units sold, BEP 75

Actual revenue (300 × R1 800)540 000
BEP revenue (75 × R1 800)(135 000)
Margin of Safety (rands)405 000
MoS %75%
ShoeShine's 75% MoS means sales can drop by 75% before the business loses money — they're selling 4× their break-even volume. Extremely safe.
6.5Sensitivity Analysis — Should We Change Something?▼

Sensitivity (what-if) analysis evaluates proposed changes: advertising campaigns, price changes, volume changes. The approach is always the same:

1
Calculate total contribution at the new volume with any new selling price
2
Subtract all fixed costs including any new costs (advertising budget)
3
Compare profit/loss to the original scenario
4
If profit improves → implement the change. If not → don't.

Example: MacBurgers advertising campaign (R90k cost, +8 000 units)

Without adWith ad

Decision: Yes — advertise. Changes a R50k loss into a R20k profit.

New contribution: R115 − R100= R15/unit

Unit 7 — Cash Budgets
Budgetary control · Cash flow planning · Receivables schedules · Liquidity management

Why Cash Budgets Matter

A business can be profitable on paper but still run out of cash. The income statement shows profit — but profit includes credit sales not yet collected and ignores loan repayments. The cash budget shows what actually hits the bank account, and when.

Management uses cash budgets to: know when to invest surplus cash, know when to arrange borrowing before a shortfall happens, and ensure enough cash for day-to-day operations.

7.1Cash vs Non-Cash — The Most Important Distinction▼

A cash budget only includes actual cash movements. Anything that affects profit but doesn't move cash is excluded.

✅ Cash Items (include)
Cash sales
Debtor collections (when cash received, not when sale made)
Wages and salaries paid
Rent payments
Electricity payments
Creditor payments
Petty cash
Interest paid/received
Asset purchases/sales for cash
❌ Non-Cash Items (exclude)
Depreciation — never in cash budget
Credit sales (until cash received)
Accruals and provisions
Bad debt write-offs
Inventory movements (tracked on balance sheet)
Depreciation is the single most-tested exclusion. It's an expense on the income statement but involves no cash movement. Many students instinctively include it — don't.

7.3Receivables Collection Schedule — Step by Step▼

When customers buy on credit, the cash arrives in later months. You need a collection schedule to know how much arrives when.

Jabu Traders credit terms:

  • 20%Pay in the month of sale — but receive a 10% discount. So you get: 20% × 90% = 18% of the credit sale amount.
  • 50%Pay in month 1 after the sale — no discount, full amount.
  • 30%Pay in month 2 after the sale — no discount, full amount.
The discount applies only to current-month payers. The 50% and 30% groups pay the full credit sale amount.

7.4Creditor Payments — Timing Is Everything▼

When you buy on credit, you pay in a later month. The key question is: when does the cash actually leave?

Account purchases (credit)

Paid in the month following the purchase.

Oct creditor payment = Sept purchases
Sept: Wood R80k + Screws R3k = R83 000

Nov creditor payment = Oct purchases
Oct: Wood R90k + Screws R4k = R94 000

Petty cash purchases

Paid in the same month as purchased — no credit.

Sept petty cash: R3 600 → paid in Sept
Oct petty cash: R3 600 → paid in Oct
Nov petty cash: R4 000 → paid in Nov
7.5The 13th Cheque — November Salary Trap▼

This is one of the most commonly tested traps. In November, staff receive a 13th cheque — an additional month's salary as a year-end bonus. This means the salary payment in November is double the normal amount.

November Salary Line
Normal salary × 2 = November cash payment
eg: R123 000 × 2 = R246 000
Jabu Traders trap: The slides show October salaries as R123 000 but November as R246 000. The original slide showed R244 000 — slight rounding difference. Always use the data given in your exam question.

7.7Liquidity, Solvency & the Companies Act▼
Liquidity
Current Assets > Current Liabilities

The ability to pay debts as they fall due — short-term cash health. A business can be profitable but illiquid if its cash is tied up in slow-moving debtors or stock.
Solvency
Total Assets > Total Liabilities

Overall financial health — not just short-term. A business could be liquid (enough cash this month) but insolvent (total debts exceed total assets).
Companies Act 2008: A company must be BOTH liquid AND solvent both before and after any dividend payments to shareholders. This was a key legislative update — directors must apply both tests, not just one.
